PART I: SYSTEM OVERVIEW, BRAND IDENTITY & CORE PRINCIPLES

1.1 Background, Purpose of the Invention, and Brand Identity

Trees Pinyin™ is an original, proprietary English-based phonetic annotation and transliteration system invented specifically for native English speakers (and learners educated in English phonics) to master Spoken Mandarin Chinese with immediate, intuitive accuracy.

  • Visual Trademark & Brand Identity (TREES PINYIN™): The Trees Pinyin system is officially represented by its graphic trademark featuring a lush, layered green tree canopy over a sturdy brown trunk, emblazoned with the capitalized words "TREES PINYIN" in warm golden-yellow rounded typography outlined in dark green. Just as a flourishing tree branches out into thousands of leaves from a single solid trunk, the Trees Pinyin system enables learners to branch out into thousands of Mandarin characters and everyday phrases from the solid, familiar trunk of English phonics intuition. Official audio resources, web applications, browser extensions, and published learning series are hosted under the official brand domain www.treespinyin.com.
  • Technical Problem Solved: Standard Hanyu Pinyin (the official Romanization system for Mandarin) utilizes numerous Roman letters in ways that directly contradict English phonics conventions—such as q, x, zh, c, ü, i, e, ou, ui, and ian. When native English speakers read Hanyu Pinyin using their native reading instincts, severe pronunciation distortions and cognitive friction occur.
  • Technical Solution: Trees Pinyin systematically deconstructs Mandarin phonology and maps every initial, final, and overall syllable onto intuitive English letter combinations and connected speech patterns. By integrating proprietary "Silent Articulatory Guides ( )", a "Non-Rhotic British Vowel Standard", and "Inherent Vocal Resonance Rules", learners can bypass the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) and complex linguistic training—relying purely on their native English reading intuition and oral muscle coordination to produce authentic Mandarin sounds within a single beat.

1.2 Four Core Pedagogical & Design Pillars

1. Intuitive Phonics & Connected Speech

All phonetic symbols are constructed from familiar English letter combinations or natural English connected speech blends. Learners simply sound them out based on native intuition.

2. Silent Articulatory Guides ( )

Letters enclosed in bold-italic parentheses—such as (r)th, (r)ds, or ee(oo)—are audibly silent, but instruct the speaker to pre-position or maintain the exact mouth shape or tongue posture while vocalizing the adjacent sound.

3. Monosyllabic Blending (One Beat)

Every Chinese character is strictly monosyllabic (one single beat). In mnemonic sentences, bolded and underlined English word parts blend continuously into a single syllable (e.g., Let's see an → tseean for 千 / qiān, "Thousand").

4. Contextual Tones & Number Anchors

Speak out loud using a relaxed, fluid "hybrid" (Pidgin-style) intonation while mastering tones through the "Do" Contextual Tone Method and Number-Tone Anchor Groups (e.g., anchor 12 linking "Twelve" and "Holiday").

1.3 Mandatory Visual & Typographical Conventions

  • Bold-Italic Notation: Every Trees Pinyin transcription is formatted in bold-italics (e.g., booer, (r)s2 hoh4) to visually distinguish phonetic symbols from standard English text and Hanyu Pinyin reference text.
  • Anti-Confusion Capitalization (L): The Mandarin initial l is permanently mapped to an uppercase bold-italic L (e.g., Lah, Leeoh4) to prevent visual confusion with an uppercase I or the digit 1 in sans-serif italic fonts.
  • Anti-Confusion Hyphenation (er-n): The Mandarin final en is permanently written with a hyphen as er-n (e.g., mer-n, her-n3) to ensure that adjacent italic letters r and n (rn) are never visually mistaken for the letter m.

PART II: ARTICULATORY MECHANISMS & SPECIAL SYMBOL COMBINATIONS

The core innovation of Trees Pinyin lies in how it guides the vocal tract muscles of an English speaker to synthesize authentic Mandarin sounds through five specialized articulatory mechanisms:

2.1 Silent Guides ( ): Mouth Shape & Tongue Posture Coordination

Whenever bold-italic letters appear inside parentheses ( ), the learner must not pronounce them as independent sounds, but must lock in their physical articulatory posture (mouth shape or tongue position):

  1. The Silent Retroflex Prefix (r) (Mapping Mandarin zh, ch, sh, r):
    • Physiological Mechanism: When native English speakers prepare to pronounce an initial r (as in red or run), the tip of the tongue naturally curls upward and backward toward the hard palate (retroflexion). By placing a silent (r) before ds, ts, s, and th, Trees Pinyin instructs the speaker to first curl the tip of the tongue into the English r position (without producing the vocalic English r-glide), and while holding that curled tongue posture, immediately release the following consonant airstream:
    • (r)ds (zh): Hold the r curled-tongue posture + articulate the unaspirated ds cluster (as at the end of birds or hands). This physically synthesizes the Mandarin retroflex unaspirated affricate zh.
    • (r)ts (ch): Hold the r curled-tongue posture + articulate a strongly aspirated ts cluster (as at the end of cats). This synthesizes the Mandarin retroflex aspirated affricate ch.
    • (r)s (sh): Hold the r curled-tongue posture + release the s fricative airstream (similar to English fish, but with the tongue tip curled further back). This synthesizes the Mandarin retroflex fricative sh.
    • (r)th (r): Hold the r curled-tongue posture + vibrate the vocal cords as if pronouncing the voiced English th (/ð/, as in this or that). By combining the r tongue curl with voiced th friction, the speaker avoids the excessive lip-rounding of American English r and accurately produces the authentic Mandarin voiced retroflex r (e.g., 日 / rì → (r)th4).
  2. The Silent Labial-Rounding Guide ee(oo) (Mapping Mandarin ü / v):
    • Physiological Mechanism: The Mandarin close front rounded vowel ü (IPA: /y/) does not exist in modern English. Phonetically, it is the simultaneous combination of the tongue position of the high front vowel ee (/iː/) and the lip-rounding posture of the high back vowel oo (/uː/).
    • Synthesized Phoneme: In ee(oo), the learner audibly vocalizes the long vowel ee (as in bee or see, keeping the tongue high and forward against the lower teeth), while simultaneously rounding and protruding the lips into the exact shape required to say oo (as in moon)—without actually gliding into the oo sound. By vocalizing ee through an (oo) mouth shape, English speakers effortlessly produce a native-level Mandarin ü.
  3. The Central Vowel Transition Guide (uh)-er (Mapping Mandarin er):
    • Physiological Mechanism: For the Mandarin rhotacized final er (as in 兒/而/耳), the parenthetical (uh) instructs the speaker to start with a relaxed, neutral mid-central vowel posture (schwa /ə/) and immediately curl the tongue into an American r-colored er glide. For the fourth-tone number "Two" (二 / èr), both parts are fully vocalized and emphasized as uh-er4.
  4. Word Parentheses in English Phonetic Aids:
    • In English reference words (outside the bold-italic Trees Pinyin symbols), normal letters placed inside parentheses indicate silent segments of the English word that must be omitted when sounding out the mnemonic comparison—for example: wor(l)d (omit the l sound) or (fi)sh (omit the fi and only use the sh sound).

2.2 The Non-Rhotic British Pronunciation Standard (er and ar)

  • Core Rule: Throughout the Trees Pinyin system, vowel symbols containing the letter r—specifically er (mapping Mandarin e) and ar—follow British English (Non-Rhotic / Received Pronunciation) rules, where the final letter r is completely silent and uncurled.
  • Articulatory Effect (er): Pronounced like British English her, term, or sister (/ɜː/ or /ə/ gliding toward unrounded back /ɤ/), with a flat tongue. This prevents native English speakers from misreading the single Hanyu Pinyin letter e as English /iː/ (me) or /e/ (bed), while avoiding unwanted American r-curling on syllables like 哥 (ger / gē) or 德 (der2 / dé).
  • Sole American Rhotic Exception: Only the standalone retroflex syllable er—written as (uh)-er (for 兒/而/耳) or uh-er4 (for 二 / "Two")—uses the American English r-colored (rhotic) r sound.

2.3 Vocal Resonance & Inherent Voicing of Standalone Consonants

  • Core Principle: Every Chinese character is inherently voiced because every Mandarin syllable contains a vowel nucleus. Unlike English, where word-final sibilants and affricates (such as -s in bus, -ce in force, or -ts in cats) end in unvoiced, breathy airflow, Mandarin standalone syllables must end in a full, resonant voiced vowel.
  • Visual Omission of Apical -i: In Hanyu Pinyin, the syllables zi, ci, si, zhi, chi, shi, ri use the letter i to represent apical (tongue-tip) vowels, which English speakers invariably mispronounce as /iː/ (see) or /ɪ/ (sit). Trees Pinyin eliminates this confusion by omitting the letter i entirely, writing them as standalone symbols: ds, ts, s, (r)ds, (r)ts, (r)s, (r)th.
  • Burst-to-Resonance Transition: When pronouncing any of these standalone symbols, start with a sharp burst of air or friction (e.g., for Four / 四 / sì → s4, start like the -ce at the end of force or -s in bus; for ts, start like -ts in cats), but immediately engage the vocal cords while holding the exact same tongue position, transitioning into a strong, continuous, resonant voiced vowel until the end of the beat.

PART IV: SIX CORE SPELLING RULES & VOWEL OMISSION MECHANICS

When combining initials and finals into complete Trees Pinyin syllables, six deterministic rules govern the orthography:

Rule 1: Direct Concatenation (基礎直接拼接)

Unless modified by Rules 2 through 6 below, a Mandarin syllable is formed by directly concatenating its Trees Pinyin initial and Trees Pinyin final with no space in between:

  • b (b) + a (ah) → bah (巴)
  • b (b) + o (ooer) → booer (波)
  • d (d) + en (er-n) → der-n (扽)
  • h (h) + ua (ooah) → hooah (花)

Rule 2: Dual Contextual Mapping of Final o (oh vs. ooer)

  • Standalone Syllable / Interjection: When o appears as an independent syllable without an initial, it maps to oh (e.g., 哦 / ó → oh2).
  • Labial Initial Combinations (bo, po, mo, fo): When o follows labial initials b, p, m, f, it maps to ooer to reflect the natural lip-rounding glide: bo → booer | po → pooer | mo → mooer | fo → fooer.

Rule 3: Vowel Omission / Deduplication Mechanism (輔音帶元音消疊音機制)

Core Algorithmic Rule: When an initial that inherently contains or implies the vowel letters ee—specifically q (tsee), x (see), and j (j / inherent jee)—combines with any final beginning with ee or i, the duplicate ee must be automatically omitted to prevent redundant letter strings (such as tseeee, seeee, seeng, or jeeeer).

Initial q (tsee) Series

  • qi: tsee + ee → tsee (NOT tseeee)
  • qie: tsee + eer → tseer (NOT tseeeer)
  • qia: tsee + eeah → tseeah
  • qian: tsee + eean → tseean (千 qiān)
  • qiao: tsee + eeaw → tseeaw
  • qiu: tsee + eeoh → tseeoh
  • qin: tsee + een → tseen
  • qing: tsee + ing → tsing (ee omitted)
  • qiong: tsee + eer-oong → tseer-oong
  • qu (qü): tsee + ee(oo) → tsee(oo)
  • que (qüe): tsee + ee(oo)er → tsee(oo)er
  • quan (qüan): tsee + ee(oo)an → tsee(oo)an
  • qun (qün): tsee + ee(oo)n → tsee(oo)n

Initial x (see) Series

  • xi: see + ee → see (NOT seeee)
  • xie: see + eer → seer
  • xia: see + eeah → seeah
  • xian: see + eean → seean
  • xiao: see + eeaw → seeaw
  • xiu: see + eeoh → seeoh
  • xin: see + een → seen
  • xing: see + ing → sing (NOT seeng)
  • xiong: see + eer-oong → seer-oong
  • xu (xü): see + ee(oo) → see(oo)
  • xue (xüe): see + ee(oo)er → see(oo)er
  • xuan (xüan): see + ee(oo)an → see(oo)an
  • xun (xün): see + ee(oo)n → see(oo)n

Rule 4: Locked High-Frequency Syllables & Words (特例鎖定單字與音節)

  1. 我 (wo / "I, me"): Base syllable locked as wer; with Tone 3, written as wer3 (e.g., 我的 wǒ de → wer3 der).
  2. 也 / 耶 / 爺 / 夜 (ye): Base syllable locked as yer; "also" (也 yě) is written as yer3 (e.g., 也是 yě shì → yer3 (r)s4).
  3. 一 (yi / "One"): Base syllable locked as yee (with tone numbers added dynamically when tone sandhi applies, e.g., 一定 yí dìng → yee2 ding4).
  4. 兒 / 而 / 耳 (er) vs. 二 (èr / "Two"): General er words are locked as (uh)-er (e.g., 兒 (uh)-er2), whereas the fourth-tone number "Two" (二 èr) is locked without parentheses as uh-er4 (e.g., 十二 shí èr → (r)s2 uh-er4).

Rule 5: Hyphen Anti-Confusion Rule (er-n, er-ng, er-oong, eer-oong)

A hyphen - is strictly inserted inside the finals en (er-n), eng (er-ng), ong (er-oong), iong (eer-oong), and er ((uh)-er / uh-er4). Writing er-n (as in 們 mer-n, 很 her-n3, 問 wer-n4) prevents the adjacent italic letters r and n (rn) from visually merging into the letter m.

Rule 6: Retroflex Prefix (r) & Apical Vowel Omission (zhi, chi, shi, ri, zi, ci, si)

The retroflex initials zh, ch, sh, r always carry the silent prefix (r), mapping to (r)ds, (r)ts, (r)s, (r)th. When combined with the apical vowel -i in overall syllables (zhi, chi, shi, ri), the letter i is omitted, leaving (r)ds, (r)ts, (r)s, (r)th. Likewise, flat-tongue sibilants zi, ci, si omit i and are written simply as ds, ts, s.

PART V: TONE NOTATION SYSTEM & MNEMONIC ANCHORS

5.1 Four Tones & Neutral Tone Notation Rules

  • Tone 1 (High and steady —) & Neutral Tone (Weak form): Omitted for brevity. Any unmarked Trees Pinyin syllable is pronounced in Tone 1 (e.g., 都 dū → doo) or as a soft, short neutral tone in grammatical particles, suffixes, and reduplicated words (e.g., 哥哥 gē ge / "Elder brother" → ger ger).
  • Tone 2 (Rising /): Marked with 2 at the end of the syllable (e.g., 讀 dú → doo2; 哦 ó → oh2).
  • Tone 3 (Dipping and rising v): Marked with 3 at the end of the syllable (e.g., 堵 dǔ → doo3; 我 wǒ → wer3).
  • Tone 4 (Falling emphatically \): Marked with 4 at the end of the syllable (e.g., 度 dù → doo4; 四 sì → s4).

5.3 Number-Tone Anchor Mnemonic System (數字聲調錨點記憶系統)

Canonical Example — Anchor 12 (Tone 2 + Tone 4):

  • Twelve (十二 / shí èr / 12) is pronounced (r)s2 uh-er4 (literally (r)s2 "ten" + uh-er4 "two", carrying a Tone 2 + Tone 4 melody).
  • The number 12 naturally evokes "Holiday" because it represents December (the 12th month) and the traditional "Twelve Days of Christmas" holiday song.
  • In Mandarin, "Holiday" (節日 / jié rì) is pronounced jeer2 (r)th4—sharing the exact same Tone 2 + Tone 4 melody as "Twelve" (12).
  • Therefore, the entry is annotated as: jeer2 (r)th4 (holiday/節日/jié rì/12). All vocabulary phrases sharing the Tone 2 + Tone 4 pattern are tagged with /12) and chained into memorable sentences.
